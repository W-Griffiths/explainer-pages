What the Pentagon says about open source

From the US Department of Defense's official guide to open source software and its 2022 software policy.

“Doesn't hiding source code automatically make software more secure?”

No.

“Making source code available to the public significantly aids defenders and not just attackers … if only the attacker has the source code, the attacker ends up with another advantage.”

The UK government agrees: “Open code can be just as secure or more secure than closed code.”

“[Banning open source software] would have immediate, broad, and in some cases strongly negative impacts on the ability of the DoD to analyze and protect its own networks against hostile intrusion.”

MITRE study for the Pentagon, 2003.

“Does the [Department of Defense] already use open source software?”

Yes, extensively.

The risks of failing to consider open source:

“…increased cost, increased schedule, and/or reduced performance (including reduced innovation or security).”

The Pentagon's 2022 policy for new software:

“…preferentially adopting existing government or [open source] solutions before buying proprietary.”

“Open source is ubiquitous, it's unavoidable… having a policy against open source is impractical and places you at a competitive disadvantage.”
Mark Driver, Gartner analyst, quoted in the Department of Defense's guide

The detail

The headline figures

What open source is: software whose code is published so anyone can read, use and improve it. It's often free to use, and many companies sell paid support for it, just as they do for commercial software.

“Only 2% of the codebases we audited contained no open source components… For practical purposes, if you're building software today, you're building with open source.”
Black Duck, 2026 Open Source Security and Risk Analysis Report

The same audit found the average application contains 1,180 open source components, up 30% in a year.

The audit covered 947 commercial products that companies paid to have checked. It isn't a random sample, and software companies are over-represented.

Harvard researchers estimate that companies would spend about 3.5 times more on software if open source didn't exist, or roughly $8.8 trillion if every company built its own copy. A study for the European Commission estimated that about €1 billion invested in open source in 2018 added €65–95 billion to the EU economy.

Both are estimates from economic models. The Harvard paper is a working paper using 2020 data, and its authors call the 3.5× figure a rough, “back of the envelope” calculation.

More from the Pentagon's guide to open source

The Department of Defense publishes a long question-and-answer guide to open source software for its staff and contractors. More of its answers:

On security

“Continuous and broad peer-review, enabled by publicly available source code, improves software reliability and security through the identification and elimination of defects that might otherwise go unrecognized by the core development team.”
“In general, ‘Security by Obscurity’ is widely denigrated.”
“Some [open source software] is very secure, while others are not; some proprietary software is very secure, while others are not. Each product must be examined on its own merits.”

On how much the Pentagon relies on it

The 2003 MITRE study cited in the guide found that Pentagon security “depends on (OSS) applications and strategies.” The Department's 2022 policy calls open source “the bedrock of the software-defined world.”

On who writes it

“[Open source software] is developed by a wide variety of software developers, and the average developer is quite experienced. A Boston Consulting Group study found that the average age of [open source] developers was 30 years old, the majority had training in information technology and/or computer science, and on average had 11.8 years of computer programming experience.”

On open source as commercial software

The guide says “nearly all [open source software] is ‘commercial computer software’ as defined in US law.” For government agencies, it adds that “ignoring [open source software] would not be lawful,” because US law requires them to consider commercial software.

The guide is an explainer for staff, not formal policy, and it says it “does not constitute legal advice.” The Gartner quote dates from November 2010, and the MITRE study from 2003.

More from NASA and the military

NASA

  • Mars. The Ingenuity helicopter made 72 flights on Mars. Its software architect: “It's kind of an open-source victory, because we're flying an open-source operating system and an open-source flight software framework.”
  • Spacecraft. NASA's own spacecraft software, given away free, “has powered 40+ small to large class NASA missions including the Roman Space Telescope.”
  • Funding. In 2024 NASA put $15.6 million into maintaining free tools it relies on: “These projects are integral to our missions, critical to our data infrastructure.”

Defense and intelligence

  • Air Force. On 22 September 2020 a U-2 spy plane flew with open source software running on its onboard computers, “the first time Kubernetes has flown on an operational major weapon system in the Department of Defense.”
  • NSA. In 2019 the NSA released Ghidra, its in-house tool for analyzing software, as open source. “There have been over one million public downloads in the past four years.” The NSA also wrote SELinux, a security feature now built into Linux and “fully enforced” on Android phones.
  • Army. BRL-CAD, the Army's 3D modeling system, went open source in 2004. The Army calls it “the oldest known continuously developed open source repository in the world.”
  • Policy. The Defense Department's 2022 policy says its own non-national-security software “should be ‘open-by-default’ and released under an [open source] license unless specific conditions apply.” It calls open source “the bedrock of the software-defined world.”

Across government

Federal policy since 2016 has told agencies to judge software “free of preconceived preferences based on how the technology is developed, licensed, or distributed.” The US cyber defense agency says open source supports “every single critical infrastructure sector.”

What the biggest companies build

Google

  • Chromium is the open source project that Chrome and Edge are built on. Google says it is also used in “Bloomberg terminals and SpaceX capsule control software,” and that Google made about 94% of contributions to it in 2024. Chromium-based browsers account for about 79% of web browsing.
  • Android is open source.
  • Kubernetes is Google's system for running software across large numbers of servers. Google gave it to a new foundation in 2015, whose founding members included IBM, Intel, Cisco and Goldman Sachs.

Meta

Meta's CEO: “we benefited from the ecosystem's innovations by open sourcing leading tools like PyTorch, React, and many more tools. This approach has consistently worked for us.” He also said Meta “saved billions of dollars” by sharing its data center designs. Microsoft rebuilt Teams with React. PyTorch is now run by a foundation hosted by the Linux Foundation.

Microsoft

  • TypeScript became the most used language on GitHub, by number of contributors, in August 2025.
  • .NET has been open source since 2014. The code behind VS Code is open source, and Microsoft adds its own branding on top.
  • Microsoft also owns GitHub, the main home of open source code.

Apple

“Open source software is at the heart of Apple platforms and developer tools.” The core of macOS and iOS is open source , as is WebKit, the engine inside Safari.

Pixar

Pixar made its 3D scene format open source in 2016. In 2023 Pixar, Adobe, Apple, Autodesk and NVIDIA formed the Alliance for OpenUSD to develop it as an open standard.

Chrome, Edge, Safari, macOS and VS Code are themselves commercial products. The open source parts are the foundations they're built on.

Is it free to use?

Usually, yes, including in commercial products. The most common open source license on GitHub is the MIT license. Its key sentence:

“Permission is hereby granted, free of charge, to any person obtaining a copy of this software and associated documentation files (the “Software”), to deal in the Software without restriction, including without limitation the rights to use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, and/or sell copies of the Software…”
The MIT License

The main condition is that the copyright notice stays with the software. The Apache 2.0 license, the second most common, grants a “no-charge, royalty-free, irrevocable” license, plus a license to any related patents held by the people who contributed the code.

Paying is optional. Companies pay for support, hosting or extra features, not for the right to use the code. For example, Microsoft sells a fully managed service that “runs the community version of PostgreSQL.”

Paid support

Microsoft sells a fully managed service that “runs the community version of PostgreSQL,” an open source database. Amazon offers the same. Canonical sells up to 15 years of maintenance and “24/7 support” for Ubuntu Linux.

Other evidence on security

Security experts have long held that a system should stay secure even if everyone knows how it works. Encryption, which protects everything from online banking to email, works this way: the methods are published openly so that experts everywhere can test them.

“Open code can be just as secure or more secure than closed code. For example, cryptographic algorithms are open and prove their robustness through peer review.”
UK Government Digital Service, 2017
“There is no empirical evidence that the particular type of software development is the primary driver of security. Rather, the policy of the particular vendor determines the patching behavior.”
Peer-reviewed study of 17 widely used products, Communications of the ACM, 2011

The idea dates from 1883, when the cryptographer Auguste Kerckhoffs wrote that a security system must stay safe even if it falls “into the hands of the enemy.”

Who writes and funds it

In Linux 7.2 (August 2026), the top contributors were Intel, Google, Red Hat, AMD and Qualcomm. Developers with no employer made 4.5% of changes. The rest came from other employers, or couldn't be matched to one (16.7%).

The Linux Foundation's top-tier members include Microsoft, Google, Meta, Oracle, Intel and Samsung, among nearly 2,000 members. The Apache Software Foundation's top sponsors include Apple, Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft and Visa.

Terms
Open source
Software whose code is published so anyone can read, use and improve it, under a license that allows this.
Proprietary, closed source
Software whose code is kept private by the company that owns it.
Source code
The human-readable instructions that make up a program.
Linux
An open source operating system, doing the same job as Windows. It runs most servers and cloud services, and Android is built on it.
